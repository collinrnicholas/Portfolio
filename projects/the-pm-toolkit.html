Home / Work / The PM Toolkit

A product I would have paid for at work.

The PM Toolkit is a grounded AI workspace for product managers. It carries a project from market research and customer insight through strategy, PRDs, ROI modelling, roadmaps, and the executive summary that has to survive a board review. I designed, built, and shipped it alone — it runs in production, bills real customers, and has users I have never met.

01 · The problem

Product work is fragmented, and the artefacts are the deliverable

A working PM keeps context across a dozen tools and produces a specific set of artefacts: a market scan, a customer insight synthesis, a strategy narrative, a PRD, a financial model, a roadmap, and an executive summary that has to hold up in a room full of people who will poke at it.

The gap I kept hitting is that the tools are either blank-page generators (fast, but you cannot defend the output) or heavyweight platforms that assume a research team. The product I wanted was one that made the work faster without making the reasoning weaker — every generated artefact anchored to the project’s own brief, research, and uploaded files rather than to generic training-data patterns.

I had a second motive, honestly. I wanted to prove that the methodology I was advocating at work — that a product person can ship a real product with AI — held up outside a supportive enterprise environment, with billing, auth, and real strangers using it.

02 · Product decisions

Grounded output over impressive output

The central product decision was to make synthesis consume the project’s own material. When a brief is written or a file is uploaded, the system builds a working profile of the ideal customer and stores it on the project; every downstream artefact is constructed against that profile. The output is narrower and more specific than a generic generator’s — which is exactly the point, because specific is what makes it usable in a real review.

Structure
Phases — research, strategy, financials — each ending in a synthesis that consolidates what was learned before the next phase begins.
Decision log
A first-class decisions record with resolution paths (decide, validate, defer, descope, mitigate, accept) and append-only chains, so a superseded decision is never silently deleted.
Artefacts
PRD, roadmap, epics and user stories, launch plan, pitch, ROI model, executive summary — each one editable, versioned, and exportable.
Voice
A realtime voice mode, so a PM can think out loud through a problem instead of typing into a form.
Instrumentation
Product analytics from day one, and a public feature surface indexed for discovery — because a product nobody finds has no users.

Deliberately out of scope: real-time collaboration, enterprise SSO, and anything requiring a sales conversation. Each one would have been a reasonable feature and each one would have delayed the only question that mattered, which was whether the core artefact quality was good enough that someone would pay for it.

03 · What building it alone changed

The part that transfers back to enterprise product work

Building this end to end — data model, prompt architecture, billing, onboarding, analytics, SEO, support — removed the abstraction layer I used to have when talking to engineers. I now know where the cost actually lives: it is rarely the feature, and almost always the grounded data path behind it. That changes how I scope, and it changes what I am willing to promise in a roadmap review.

It also changed how I run AI initiatives. Evaluation stops being a phase and becomes a habit. When you are the person who gets the support email about a bad output, prompt quality stops being a research topic and becomes a product requirement with an owner.

04 · Outcome

In production, with strangers using it

The PM Toolkit is live, subscription-billed, and used by product managers who found it without me in the room. That is the whole claim: not that a prototype was demoed, but that a product was shipped, priced, launched, instrumented, and operated — by one person, with AI as the multiplier rather than the product.

It is also the most direct evidence I can offer for how I would run an AI product organisation: small loops, working software over alignment decks, evidence over confidence, and a hard line between what the model is allowed to decide and what a person decides.
