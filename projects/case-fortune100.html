Recovering a Fortune 100 account.

A failed integration after an acquisition left a major customer ready to terminate. The recovery took six months, four functions, and one uncomfortable admission.

Role
Product lead, retention recovery
Company
Jobvite → Employ
Outcome
$880K · two-year agreement
01 · Business context

A strategic account, mid-migration.

A Fortune 100 customer ran a large portion of its hiring through our platform. When the business combined products after an acquisition, that customer was moved onto a shared integration path. It broke.

This was not a bug with a workaround. Hiring workflows the customer had built their process around stopped behaving the way they had been promised, and their team had already escalated to executive level.

02 · Strategic problem

The contract gave them an easy exit.

The agreement included termination for convenience. The customer could leave without cause, and they were preparing to. Retention was not the only exposure: this was a reference account in their sector, and losing it would have cost more than the ARR.

The technical problem was tractable. The commercial problem was trust, and trust was running out on their timeline, not ours.

03 · Options and trade-offs

Three responses, and what each one cost.

  1. Hold the migrationSafest technically. It confirmed the customer's fear that we could not deliver what we had sold.
  2. Revert them to the old pathRestored service immediately, but split the platform strategy and created a permanent exception.
  3. Fix forward with a named teamHighest short-term cost in engineering focus. The only option that kept the customer, the platform, and the reference intact.
04 · Decision and rationale

Fix forward, and say the hard part first.

We chose to fix forward. My reasoning was that a temporary exception would have solved this quarter's contract and created next year's architecture problem, while also signalling that the customer's own migration was negotiable.

The first decision was not technical. I told the customer plainly that we had broken their workflow, what we were changing, and what would be true by a specific date. That admission bought the time the engineering work required.

05 · My leadership role

Leading across four functions and an executive relationship.

The product recovery was mine to lead: prioritization against the rest of the roadmap, the technical plan with engineering, and the sequencing that stabilized the customer's critical workflows first. Six months of working the relationship with Sales and Customer Success, and writing the executive updates the customer's leadership read.

Engineering did not report to me, the account team did not report to me, and the renewal was not mine to sign. The work was alignment under pressure: one priority, one story, one date.

06 · Measurable outcomes

Retained, expanded, and locked in.

The customer stayed. They renewed at $440K ARR, up from about $271K, on a two-year, $880K enterprise agreement — a 1.6× expansion rather than a save.

They also dropped the termination-for-convenience clause. That was the truest measure of the outcome: the exit got harder because the confidence came back.

07 · Lessons and reflections

What I took from it.

Integration work is customer-facing strategy, not plumbing. The failure was not that a migration went badly; it was that we treated a strategic account's migration as an internal sequencing decision.

I now ask, before any platform consolidation, which customer would feel it first and what we owe them before the change lands. That question is cheaper to answer at design time than in a recovery meeting.
