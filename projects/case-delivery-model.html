Home/Work/Delivery model

Changing how the team ships.

Releases went out every few months. The bottleneck looked like engineering; it turned out to be a sequence of constraints, each hiding the next one.

Role
Director of Product · operating model
Company
Persado
Outcome
Weekly releases, up from every few months
01 · Business context

A product organization that could not release often.

Persado's product and engineering teams worked on long cycles. Releases went out every few months, which meant customer feedback arrived late, priorities drifted between planning and delivery, and every launch carried too much change at once.

The team also had no single roadmap. Work was sequenced by whoever had the loudest immediate need.

02 · Strategic problem

The cadence was the product problem.

Slow releases were not a delivery inconvenience; they shaped what the product could be. Experiments took a quarter to learn from. Pricing and packaging changes could not be tested. Sales had no predictable date to sell against.

The strategic constraint was time-to-learning. Fixing it meant changing how the organization worked, not adding capacity.

03 · Options and trade-offs

Add people, add process, or remove friction.

  1. Add engineering capacityExpensive, slow to hire, and it would have accelerated a process that still released quarterly.
  2. Add a delivery processFamiliar and safe, but more ceremony around the same bottlenecks tends to produce more meetings, not more releases.
  3. Remove the friction, then re-measureLeast visible work. Required admitting that the constraint would move once the first one was removed.
04 · Decision and rationale

Fix the constraint, then find the next one.

The choice was to attack friction directly, in sequence. With engineering leadership I introduced stand-ups, simplified the Jira workflows that were adding handoffs without adding information, and established the team's first roadmap so priorities stopped changing mid-cycle. Then we set a weekly Wednesday deploy.

The bet was that cadence would create its own demand for discipline. It did, and the constraint moved: once releases were weekly, the bottleneck became marketing and enablement, because the field could not absorb changes it had not been told about.

05 · My leadership role

Direct ownership of product, influence everywhere else.

Product and design report to me at Persado, so prioritization and the roadmap were mine to set. The deploy cadence, Jira simplification, and engineering rituals were built jointly with engineering leadership; that team did not report to me.

The second half was pure influence. I added release messaging in Slack and email, and I now lead enablement sessions for sales, marketing, and customers. None of that existed before, and none of it was in my reporting line.

06 · Measurable outcomes

Weekly, and the constraint moved.

Releases now ship weekly rather than every few months. The team has a roadmap it can prioritize against, and launch communication reaches sales, marketing, and customers before the change lands rather than after.

This is early work — I joined in August 2026 — so I am reporting the operating change and the cadence, not revenue attribution.

07 · Lessons and reflections

Constraints are sequential, not singular.

Every operating model has a bottleneck, and removing one exposes the next. The mistake most teams make is declaring victory at the first release cadence improvement and then wondering why adoption did not follow.

What I would do differently: instrument the enablement side earlier. We learned that the field was the real constraint only after the cadence changed, and we could have anticipated it.
