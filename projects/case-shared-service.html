Home/Work/Shared service

Creating a $1.76M revenue stream.

Three ATS platforms, three fragmented integrations, and a market moving toward programmatic job advertising. The decision to build one commercial service instead of three.

Role
Product Manager · platform architecture and commercial model
Company
Jobvite · Jobvite, Lever & JazzHR
Outcome
$1.76M ARR · first year
01 · Business context

Three products, three ways to do the same job.

Jobvite, Lever, and JazzHR served overlapping customers with separate codebases. Programmatic job distribution — posting a role and buying traffic against it — existed in each product as its own integration, built at different times, with different vendors and different commercial terms.

Customers bought the same outcome three ways. The business paid for it three times.

02 · Strategic problem

The cost of the third build.

Every new network, every pricing change, and every customer-specific requirement had to be implemented three times. Product scope grew with the number of platforms rather than the number of customers.

The strategic question was not whether to integrate. It was whether integration was a feature per product, or a platform capability the portfolio could price and sell.

03 · Options and trade-offs

Three paths, one price tag.

  1. Rebuild in each productFastest for the loudest customer, and the most expensive over the portfolio's life. Three roadmaps, three QA surfaces, three billing models.
  2. Buy a vendor's turnkey productLeast engineering, least differentiation. It also left pricing and packaging in the vendor's hands.
  3. Build one shared serviceHigher upfront design cost, one integration surface, and a commercial model we could own. Required alignment across three product teams and two external partners.
04 · Decision and rationale

One service, priced as a product.

I chose the shared service. The deciding factor was not engineering efficiency; it was commercial control. A reusable integration let the business define packaging, price, and terms once, then sell the same capability across every product line.

I designed the architecture with VONQ and LinkedIn, keeping the vendor-facing layer modular so a new network could be added without touching each product. Pricing and the commercial model were designed alongside the platform, not retrofitted after launch — the difference between selling an integration and selling a product.

05 · My leadership role

Product ownership across a portfolio I didn't manage directly.

I owned the product decision end to end: the business case, the architecture, the vendor relationships, the commercial model, and the rollout across three product teams that did not report to me.

The influence work was the job. Each platform team had its own roadmap and its own customers. Alignment came from a shared commercial case — one service, one price, revenue attributed to each product — rather than from an org chart.

06 · Measurable outcomes

$1.76M in year one.

The service produced $1.76M in first-year recurring revenue and replaced three separate integration paths with one. New networks and pricing changes shipped once and became available everywhere.

It also set the pattern the portfolio reused later: build the platform capability once, then price it as a product rather than a feature.

07 · Lessons and reflections

What I would do differently.

I would bring finance into the pricing model earlier. The commercial design worked, but the attribution conversation across three product P&Ls happened later than it needed to, and that delay cost momentum in the rollout.

The durable lesson: shared services win on economics, not on elegance. Make the money legible to every team that has to give up roadmap to support it.
