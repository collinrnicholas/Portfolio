Home / Work / BidPacket

The liability boundary is the product.

BidPacket is a pay-per-use tool for contractors: upload an architectural plan set or an RFP, and receive a clean, organized bid preparation packet. It is deliberately not an estimating engine, and the hardest product work was deciding — and then writing down — exactly what it would never claim to do.

01 · The problem

The work before the estimate is the work nobody wants

A contractor chasing a commercial job receives a fifty-to-two-hundred-page plan set or RFP and, before any pricing can start, has to do something entirely different: work out what is actually being asked for. Which sheets matter, which divisions are in scope, what the submission requirements are, which addenda changed something, and where the ambiguities are that will come back as a change order later.

That preparation is unpaid, unglamorous, and repetitive — and skipping it is how contractors lose money on jobs they win. The existing software market is built for the estimating half of the problem and priced as a monthly platform. A small contractor bidding a handful of jobs a month neither needs nor wants another subscription.

The opening was the gap before estimation, sold the way the customer already buys things: per job.

02 · The defining decision

Draw the liability line first, then build to it

Every AI product in a regulated or consequential domain has one decision that determines whether it can exist at all, and it is usually made badly: how far does the automation go, and who is responsible when it is wrong?

For a construction bid, the consequence of a wrong number is a lost job or a contract signed underwater. So the line is explicit and enforced in the product, not buried in terms of service:

It does
Organize the documents, extract and structure what the plan set specifies, surface the submission requirements, flag scope and ambiguity, and assemble a bid preparation packet.
It does not
Produce pricing, quantities, compliance determinations, or the submission itself. Those remain the contractor’s call, and the product says so on the page.
Why it matters
A narrower, defensible claim is worth more than a broad one the product cannot back. It also removed an entire class of support and legal risk before a line of the pipeline was written.

The same discipline shows up in the pricing: a single price per document with no subscription. Nothing about the business model requires a customer to stay, which keeps the incentive aligned with the packet being good enough on its own.

03 · Build approach

Plan the whole system before writing the pipeline

I ran this project the opposite way to how most side projects start: the planning artefacts and the landing page came first, and the product pipeline second. Not out of process worship — because the two questions that kill a product like this (is the liability boundary defensible, and will a contractor pay per document) can both be answered before building anything.

Architecture first
Data model, order flow, service boundaries, and unit economics written down as the source of truth before any structural code.
Copy as a constraint
The voice and liability language are governed by a written rules file that applies to every user-facing string and every generated output, so the boundary cannot erode one convenient sentence at a time.
Design system as tokens
No hard-coded colour, size, radius, or shadow anywhere — every value comes from a token file, so the interface cannot drift as it grows.
Commercial spine
Checkout, delivery, and analytics chosen and wired by design — payment per document, packet delivered by email, and the funnel instrumented from the first visitor.
04 · A judgement call worth naming

The working prototype stays off the internet

The public product grew out of a single-user local tool I had already built. It has no authentication of any kind, which means anyone who could reach it could upload files, read every project and generated document, see the price list, and spend my model credits.

So it is deliberately not wired to the deploy path. It is not “temporarily” unprotected and it is not waiting on a hardening sprint — it is structurally incapable of being published by accident, because the start command that ships the landing page physically cannot serve it. I have watched more than one company learn this lesson in production. The port is deliberate: the document-generation core is worth keeping, and the server, client, and storage layers get replaced.

05 · What it demonstrates

Scoping by exclusion

BidPacket is the clearest example of how I scope a 0→1 bet: decide what the product will not do, make that visible to the customer, and let the narrow claim be the thing that earns trust. It is the same instinct behind the shared-service architecture at Employ and the evidence contract in Arclo — find the boundary that removes an entire class of risk, then build inside it.

It is currently in build: architecture, design system, liability language, and landing page complete; pipeline, payments, and delivery next.
