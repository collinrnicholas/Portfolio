How I think about product.

Seven things I believe about product investment, evidence, and the job of leading.

Product investment is a business decision

Every product, strategy, or vision is a bet placed with the company's money, whether the idea comes from the CEO, a customer, or the team. The job is to improve the odds before that money is committed: research the claim, test it as cheaply as possible, decide where it belongs against everything else on the roadmap — then measure whether it paid off, in numbers the customer recognizes at renewal.

How I evaluate an opportunity

Start with the commercial question: which decision does this change, for whom, and what is that worth? Then find the assumption most likely to break and the cheapest way to test it. Ideas that survive get a roadmap slot. Ideas that don't get a clear no, because a vague maybe is the most expensive answer a product leader can give.

Short-term growth and long-term platform

Conversion work pays this quarter; platform work makes the next ten quarters cheaper. Both need deliberate funding rather than letting urgency decide. The shared service at Jobvite was platform work that became a revenue stream. The ROI dashboard at Persado was conversion work that became a retention tool.

Working across engineering, design, and commercial teams

Product is the bridge between engineering, marketing, and sales, so close leadership partners in each function matter: most delays live in the gaps between teams rather than inside them. When there is something valuable to ship, product should never be the reason a customer doesn't have it yet.

Evidence over assumption

I'd rather learn in three weeks than defend a plan for nine months. That means writing the claim down, instrumenting it, and being willing to kill the thing I championed. The recovery case is the honest version of this: we had shipped on an assumption, and the customer paid for it.

What AI changes

AI lowers the cost of finding out. Prototyping before asking engineering for a quarter gives customers and teams something specific to react to. It also raises the bar on governance: transparency, accuracy oversight, and bias review are product requirements, not paperwork.

Why I still build

The degree is a B.S. in Mechanical Engineering / Mechanical Technology from Central Connecticut State University; the habit of shipping independently never stopped. Engineering taught me that most problems are solvable once you understand the system, have the right resources, and have room to make decisions. Building keeps the trade-offs honest, because the constraints are the ones I set.
